Polarity

Each blue arrow points along a polar bond from δ+ to δ− (Pauling electronegativity difference 0.4 or more). Their vector sum on this 3D model decides polar or nonpolar, and the gold arrow shows its direction. This is the textbook sketch: lone pairs are left out and there are no numbers. The shape matters too: 1,2-dichloroethane is nonpolar anti but polar gauche.

Aromatic rings

Tap a ring to see the p orbital on each ring atom, standing up from the ring plane. Each atom adds π electrons: 1 for a double bond, 2 for a lone pair in its p orbital (pyrrole N–H, furan O), 0 for a lone pair that lies in the ring plane (pyridine N). A flat ring of p orbitals with 4n + 2 π electrons (2, 6, 10 …) is aromatic (Hückel's rule).

Equivalent H (¹H NMR)

H atoms with the same letter are chemically equivalent and give one signal. The test: swap one H for D. Swaps that give the same compound, or its mirror image (enantiotopic H), are equivalent; swaps that give diastereomers (diastereotopic H) are not. Fast motions such as CH₃ rotation and chair flips average out. O–H and N–H signals can look different in a real spectrum because those H exchange.

Molecular Visions parts

    Counts follow Dr. Dan Berger's Molecular Visions guide (Bluffton University). Your kit may differ, so check its manual. The kit builds one Lewis structure, and aromatic rings count as alternating single and double bonds, so it cannot show resonance.

    Stereocenters (R/S)

    R/S uses the CIP priority rules. Point group 4 away from you: 1 → 2 → 3 clockwise is R, counterclockwise is S.

    Double bonds and rings

    E/Z uses the CIP priority rules. cis/trans is shown for a C=C with one H on each carbon, and for ring atoms that carry one H and one other group.

    Compare two structures

    Same formula but atoms joined in a different order: constitutional isomers. Joined the same way but arranged differently in space: stereoisomers, either mirror images (enantiomers) or not (diastereomers). Turning about a single bond (a new conformation) never makes a different compound.

    Bond rotation (Newman)

    You look straight down the bond, and the front carbon is closest to you. The slider turns the back carbon. Staggered: the back bonds sit between the front bonds. Eclipsed: they line up behind them. Anti: the two groups point opposite ways (180°). Gauche: they sit 60° apart. A pink rod joins two groups (not H) that sit gauche or closer and crowd each other: that crowding is steric (van der Waals) strain. A C=C or benzene-ring group gets a rod only when eclipsed. Rods are shown on bonds that get a curve.

    Listed: C–C single bonds outside rings where both carbons are sp³. Bonds that match by symmetry are listed once.

    Chair (axial / equatorial)

    Listed: six-membered rings of sp³ atoms that are chairs in this model. Axial bonds run straight up or down, parallel to the ring's axis. Equatorial bonds point outward around the ring's edge. Each ring carbon has one of each. A pink rod joins an axial group (not H) to each axial atom two ring atoms away on the same face: they crowd each other, and that is 1,3-diaxial steric strain.

    Ring angles (angle strain)

    Each ring angle is measured on this 3D model, between the two ring bonds at that atom. The further the angles sit from the ideal (109.5° for sp³, 120° for sp²), the more angle strain the ring holds.
